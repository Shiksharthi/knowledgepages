A Field Guide for Leaders

Seventeen Bearings for Aligning
Your Team With Vision & Values

A team without a shared bearing drifts — busy, but not necessarily headed anywhere. These are seventeen practical ways leaders keep everyone rowing in the same direction.

Vision and values only mean something when they show up in daily decisions — not just in a poster on the wall. The seventeen strategies below run from foundational habits to longer-term practices, so it's easy to see where to start and where to grow.

01COMMUNICATE YOUR VISION

Communicate your vision

Does your team know the vision? When employees understand and buy in to the company's vision big things happen. Humans are wired for purpose. Including employees in the big picture enhances belonging and a sense of meaning. Communicating your vision clearly and regularly is important to ensure everyone is aligned with your goals. Need inspiration? The Business Journals Leadership Trust offers these nine ways to communicate your vision effectively and regularly to your teams.

02LEAD BY EXAMPLE

Lead by example

As a leader, you must embody the values that you want your team to exhibit. Anna Stella, founder of BBSA, a global marketing agency, told Forbes that alignment happens when bosses and managers adopt a servant leadership approach, "Instead of just hanging that vision on the wall, they should go around regularly reminding people of that vision to help them connect the dots daily."

03SET CLEAR EXPECTATIONS

Set clear expectations

Promote transparency by setting clear expectations for your team. This includes job descriptions, KPIs, and performance metrics that align with your vision and values. This clarity helps your team understand the connection between their performance and the overall company mission.

04OPEN COMMUNICATION

Foster a culture of open communication

Encouraging honest and open communication does more than create a conducive environment for dialogue and sharing ideas. It also fosters a sense of unity and a belief that we're all in it together. According to Ashwin Demera, who spearheads business strategy and expansion for Emeritus, leaders are responsible for ensuring communication reflects company values: "As leaders, it's so important to set the tone and create an open space. Listen deeply, reflect thoughtfully and reiterate values regularly at all-hands meetings."

05PROMOTE COLLABORATION

Promote collaboration

Encourage collaboration with cross-functional teams working towards common goals. It breaks down silos and promotes a shared sense of purpose, especially for remote teams. Matt Masiello, Chief Marketing Officer of BabyBuddha, advises patience when starting cross-collaboration, despite its initial messiness. The results are worth it, as it enables companies to pursue bigger, more complex projects aligned with the bigger vision.

06REGULAR TEAM MEETINGS

Hold regular team meetings

Regular team meetings are a great way to discuss progress, challenges, and align with the company mission. Connecting team goals to the bigger picture helps employees see how individual wins contribute to collective success. Harvard Business Review reminds leaders that emotional buy-in occurs when leaders explain the 'why' behind objectives and tailor messages for specific teams.

07CELEBRATE SUCCESSES

Celebrate successes

Celebrating team wins and milestones provides recognition and reminds employees that you are on track with your vision. Taking time to commemorate victories on any scale fosters a sense of company pride and a feeling of purpose. Not every celebration requires goal setting and advanced planning. Many employees would be delighted by an impromptu catered lunch or paid time off for accomplishing an important goal.

08REPEAT THE "WHY"

Say the mission until you're tired of hearing yourself say it

Leaders often say the vision once — in a kickoff deck — and assume it stuck. It didn't. People need to hear the "why" repeatedly, in different words, in different settings, before it becomes part of how they think.

In practiceSouthwest Airlines is known for having crews open shift briefings by tying the day's work back to a simple idea: getting people to what matters in their lives. New hires hear it in training, then hear it again every single shift — not as a slogan, but as context for why the job exists.
09MODEL IT FIRST

Live the values before you ask anyone else to

Teams copy what leaders do, not what they say. If "customer obsession" is a value but leadership never talks to a customer, the value quietly becomes optional for everyone else too.

In practiceA software CEO who spends two hours a month answering support tickets alongside the frontline team sends a louder signal than any all-hands speech — it shows the value is real enough to cost the CEO's own time.
10CONNECT TO PERSONAL GOALS

Draw a straight line from someone's daily task to the big picture

Most disengagement isn't disagreement — it's not seeing how a task connects to anything larger. When each person's goals visibly ladder up to the team vision, the work itself feels meaningful.

In practiceInstead of a sales rep's target being just "close $50k this quarter," a manager frames it as "help 40 more small business owners get off the ground" — same number, but now it's obviously connected to the company's stated mission of supporting small businesses.
11TELL STORIES, NOT SLOGANS

Replace the mission statement with a story that proves it

A slogan is easy to forget. A specific story — with a real person, a real moment, a real outcome — sticks, and it shows the value in action instead of just describing it.

In practiceInstead of opening a weekly all-hands by reciting the values, a team lead shares a two-minute story about a support agent who stayed late to help a customer through a crisis — then simply says, "that's what we mean by 'go the extra mile.'"
12REWARD THE BEHAVIOR

Celebrate the value in action, not just the result

If bonuses and praise only go to whoever hit the biggest number, values quietly rank below results. Recognizing the behavior — even when the outcome was modest — teaches the team what actually matters.

In practiceA team gives out a peer-nominated monthly award for going out of one's way to help a colleague or customer — deliberately separate from the sales leaderboard, so effort aligned with values gets its own spotlight.
13BUILD SHARED LANGUAGE

Give the team words and rituals that carry the vision

Shared phrases and small rituals act like shorthand — they let people invoke a whole set of values in a few words, and they make the culture feel lived-in rather than imposed.

In practiceA phrase like "treat it like Day 1" becomes internal shorthand at a fast-growing company for staying scrappy and customer-focused — used in meetings without anyone needing to re-explain what it means.
14CO-CREATE THE VISION

Let the team help shape it, not just receive it

People commit more deeply to a vision they helped write. A vision handed down top-down is something to comply with; a vision built together is something to protect.

In practiceRather than leadership drafting a team charter alone, a manager runs a one-hour workshop where the team drafts their own "how we work" agreement — leadership sets the boundaries, the team fills in the specifics.
15USE VALUES AS A FILTER

Make values the tiebreaker in real decisions

Values prove themselves in trade-offs, not in easy calls. Explicitly naming which value is guiding a hard decision teaches the team how to reason the same way when you're not in the room.

In practiceIn a product review, instead of just approving or rejecting a feature, a lead asks out loud: "does this match our value of simplicity, or are we adding complexity for a small win?" — turning the value into a visible decision test.
16ADDRESS DRIFT EARLY

Name misalignment quickly, directly, and kindly

Letting a values violation slide — even a small one — teaches the whole team that the value is negotiable. Addressing it early, privately, and without drama keeps the standard intact.

In practiceWhen a manager notices a team member taking credit for a colleague's work — clashing with a stated value of transparency — they address it directly in a 1:1 the same week, rather than letting it become a pattern that others start to notice and quietly resent.
17REVISIT AND EVOLVE

Treat the vision as a living thing, not a plaque on the wall

A vision written three years ago may no longer fit today's team or market. Revisiting it periodically — with the team's input — keeps it accurate instead of becoming a piece of decoration nobody quite believes anymore.

In practiceOnce a year, a team spends part of an offsite reviewing their stated mission and values line by line, asking "does this still describe us?" and editing the wording when the team has genuinely grown or changed direction.

"Alignment isn't a single announcement — it's a hundred small course corrections, repeated until the direction becomes obvious to everyone."

A shared vision, held loosely enough to revisit and firmly enough to guide decisions, is what keeps a team moving as one.
